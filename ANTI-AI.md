# Anti-AI — the tells, and how to kill them

> Machine prose has a fingerprint. This is the catalog of it, and the antidote.
> The writing skill enforces this file; a human edit pass checks it again.
> Companion to [[WRITING.md]] (the voice). Read aloud is the final judge.

The goal is not "prose that hides it was LLM-assisted" — we disclose that openly
([[WRITING.md]] · Disclosure). The goal is prose that is **good**: specific, opinionated,
rhythmic, human. AI tells are simply the failure modes that make writing bad and bland. Kill
them and the writing gets better, not just less detectable.

---

## 0. Why machine prose reads as machine prose (the root causes)

Most tells trace to four habits. Fix the cause and dozens of symptoms vanish:

1. **It hedges instead of deciding.** No stance, no risk, endless "it's worth noting."
2. **It inflates instead of specifying.** Adjectives and abstractions stand in for a concrete
   noun, a real number, a named example.
3. **It is rhythmically flat.** Every sentence the same length; every paragraph the same shape.
4. **It is relentlessly tidy.** Three-item lists, balanced both-sides, a neat bow on every
   section. Real thought is lumpier than that.

## 1. Banned lexicon (the words that scream LLM)

**Inflated verbs — replace with plain Anglo-Saxon ones.**
delve → look at · utilize → use · facilitate → help · leverage → use · harness → use ·
foster → build · showcase → show · underscore → stress · highlight (overused) → name ·
unlock / unleash → let · empower → let · elevate → lift · navigate (metaphor) → handle ·
embark → start · spearhead → lead · boast / boasts → has.

**Inflated adjectives & nouns — usually deletable.**
vibrant, bustling, nestled, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, world-class,
game-changing, transformative, revolutionary, pivotal, crucial, vital, paramount, key (as
adj.), significant, notable, noteworthy, remarkable, profound, intricate, multifaceted,
nuanced (when unearned), holistic, comprehensive, myriad, plethora, vast, rich, powerful,
dynamic, ever-evolving, fast-paced.

**Dead metaphors.**
tapestry, landscape (the X landscape), realm, journey (the X journey), beacon, cornerstone,
bedrock, treasure trove, ecosystem (when not literal), synergy, paradigm, testament (a
testament to), at the intersection of, the rise of, a powerful tool, double-edged sword.

**Hedge & filler phrases — cut on sight.**
it's important to note / remember / consider · it's worth noting / mentioning · needless to
say · it goes without saying · that being said · when it comes to · in terms of · at the end
of the day · the bottom line is · one thing is clear · the truth is · arguably · essentially ·
basically · ultimately · in essence · for all intents and purposes.

**Transition crutches — use almost never; let logic carry the flow.**
Moreover · Furthermore · Additionally · In addition · Consequently · Therefore · Thus · Hence
· As such · That said · Indeed · Notably · Importantly · Crucially · Fundamentally.

**Conclusion tells.**
In conclusion · In summary · To sum up · All in all · As we've seen · As discussed · In essence.

**Engagement clichés (the chirpy opener).**
Let's dive in · Let's explore · Let's unpack · Let's take a closer look · Buckle up · Picture
this · Imagine a world · Have you ever wondered · Join me as · Here's the thing · Here's the
kicker · And that's not all · So, what does this mean?

**Time-setting throat-clearing.**
In today's (fast-paced / digital / modern) world · In an era of · Now more than ever · In a
world where · In recent years.

## 2. Banned constructions (templated syntax)

These sentence skeletons are the loudest tell of all. None of them, ever:

- **"It's not just X, it's Y."** / "X isn't merely Y; it's Z." / "more than just a X."
- **"Not only … but also …"**
- **"From X to Y to Z"** as a sweep ("from healthcare to finance to education").
- **"Whether you're a X or a Y, …"**
- **"X stands as a testament to Y."** / "serves as a reminder that."
- **"X plays a pivotal/crucial role in Y."**
- **"At its core, X is …"**
- **"When it comes to X, …"**
- **Rhetorical-question-then-answer:** "So what does this mean? It means…" / "The result? …"
- **"But here's the thing."** / "Here's where it gets interesting."
- **The summary apposition:** "X — a powerful tool for Y" tacked onto a noun.
- **"This is more than X. This is Y."**

## 3. Structural tells

- **The rule of three, everywhere.** Three adjectives ("fast, reliable, and scalable"),
  three-item lists, tricolon clauses. Vary list length: two, then five, then one. A single
  blunt adjective beats three padded ones.
- **Perfect parallelism.** Every bullet the same grammatical shape, every clause balanced.
  Break the pattern on purpose.
- **Symmetric both-sidesism.** "While X offers benefits, it also presents challenges." Refuses
  to weigh. (For this project: steelman *and weigh* — [[WRITING.md]] rule 8.)
- **Metronomic paragraphs.** All the same length. Use a one-sentence paragraph. Then a long one.
- **The tidy bow.** Every section ends by restating its own point. End on a concrete image or
  an honest open question instead.
- **The 5-paragraph skeleton.** Topic sentence → three supports → restatement. Visible scaffolding.
- **Over-signposting.** "First… Second… Third… Finally…" where prose should just move.
- **Restating the prompt.** "The question of whether the world is improving is a complex one."
  Delete; just answer.
- **Meta scaffolding.** "In this article, we'll explore…" / "This section covers…". Cut.
- **Noun-phrase headers with no point.** "The Importance of X", "Understanding Y", "The Role
  of Z", "Exploring the World of W". A header should make a claim or ask the real question.
- **Bolded lead-ins on every bullet** ("**Scalability:** …, **Reliability:** …") when prose
  would carry it.

## 4. Punctuation & formatting tells

- **Em-dash overuse in prose.** The signature LLM aside — like this — in every other sentence.
  Earn each one; mostly use a comma, a full stop, or a colon. (Note: the *design* uses em-dashes
  as a typographic device; that's separate from prose discipline.)
- **Title Case On Every Heading.** Use sentence case.
- **Emoji or decorative symbols in headings or body.** None.
- **Bold scattered mid-sentence for "emphasis."** Let the words carry weight.
- **Colon-introduced lists for everything.** Most lists should be sentences.
- **Numbered lists for non-sequential things.** Numbers imply order; use them only for order.
- **Curly-quote/heading flourishes** that no human editor would add.

## 5. Voice & semantic tells (the deep ones — hardest to catch)

These survive a lexical scrub. They're what make competent prose still feel hollow:

- **Hollow even-handedness.** Balanced, fluent, and says nothing. No judgment, no specifics,
  no skin in the game.
- **Abstraction over specifics.** No names, dates, places, or numbers with texture. "Many
  experts believe" instead of "Eisner's coroner records show."
- **Over-explaining the obvious.** Defining terms nobody asked about; spelling out the trivial.
- **No stance, ever.** Never surprising, never wrong, never funny, never irritated. A real
  writer occasionally takes a side, makes a joke, or admits being unsure.
- **Sycophantic cheer.** "Great question!", relentless positivity, everything "fascinating"
  and "exciting."
- **Confidently bland.** Grammatically flawless, rhythmically dead, forgettable.
- **Keyword stuffing.** Repeating the topic noun instead of using pronouns or variation.
- **False precision.** Invented specificity ("studies show a 73% increase") with no source.
  For this project that's fatal — every number traces to a cited series ([[DATA.md]]).
- **The frictionless surface.** No digressions, no texture, no rhythm changes, no voice. It
  could have been written by anyone, which means no one.
- **Vague attribution as authority.** "It is widely believed", "studies suggest", "research
  shows" with nothing behind it. Name the source or cut the claim.

## 6. The antidotes (write like this instead)

The blocklist is negative; here is the positive craft that replaces it:

- **Lead with the point** in plain words. The finding first, the wind-up never.
- **Vary the rhythm deliberately.** A very short sentence after a long one lands. Allow a
  fragment. Read aloud and listen for the metronome — break it.
- **Concrete over comprehensive.** One vivid, specific detail beats a paragraph of coverage.
  A name, a year, a number given human scale ([[WRITING.md]] rule 4).
- **Have a view, and own the uncertainty.** Say which case is stronger and why; say plainly
  where the data runs out. Confidence and doubt are both specific acts, not hedges.
- **Plain words.** Use, not utilize. Help, not facilitate. Show, not showcase. The shortest
  true word wins.
- **Cut every hedge and throat-clear.** If a sentence opens with "it's worth noting," delete
  the opener and keep the note — or delete the whole thing.
- **Let it be a little uneven.** A digression that pays off, a blunt aside, an unresolved
  ending. Human writing has texture and lumps.
- **End on the strongest concrete thing** — an image, a number, an honest tension — not a
  summary of what you just said.
- **Warmth comes from specificity,** not adjectives. "A medieval European was tens of times
  more likely to be murdered" is warm. "A truly remarkable decline" is not.

## 7. The self-check protocol (the writing skill runs this; the editor re-runs it)

After drafting, before shipping, scan for:

1. **Lexical sweep** — grep the draft against §1–§2. Any hit is rewritten, not softened.
2. **Tricolon density** — flag any sentence with three parallel items; keep only if truly needed.
3. **Em-dash density** — count per 500 words; if it's a tic, cut most.
4. **Sentence-length variance** — if most sentences are within ±5 words of each other, the prose
   is metronomic; break it up.
5. **Specificity check** — does each paragraph contain a concrete noun, a name, or a number with
   scale? If a paragraph is all abstraction, it's filler.
6. **Stance check** — is there a real claim or judgment anywhere, or is it all balanced mush?
7. **Bow check** — does any section end by restating itself? Cut the restatement.
8. **Numbers check** — does every quantitative claim trace to a cited series ([[DATA.md]])?
9. **Read-aloud** — the final test. If it sounds like a press release, a brochure, or a smart
   intern hedging, rewrite it. If it sounds like a person who actually thought about this and
   is telling you what they found — and occasionally surprises you — it passes.

---

## 8. The one test, if you remember nothing else

> Read it aloud. A human wrote it if it has a pulse: uneven rhythm, a concrete detail you can
> picture, a point of view, and at least one moment you didn't see coming. A machine wrote it
> if it's smooth, balanced, tidy, and forgettable. **Make it forgettable-proof.**
